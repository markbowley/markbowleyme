I open my laptop. 

It’s gone 8pm, the day job’s done for the day, one last email’s answered, and by any reasonable measure the day is over. 

Yet I open my laptop anyway, and spend thirty minutes on an idea that’s been turning over in my head.

Nothing gets completely finished in those thirty minutes. It’s just time poking at something that isn’t what I’m doing for the 8-10 hours of the working day.

That’s been me most weeks for the last year. Not because I had spare time, extra space or enough energy (some weeks I had neither) but because I’ve stopped waiting for the moment when I’d have all three.

Most people think you need space to start something. A clear runway. Less client work. More headspace. That one day when everything else quiets down. So they wait for it.

Over the last twelve months I’ve [built and shipped things](https://minitools.carrd.co/), [written more](https://markbowley.me/), [published regularly](https://moremaking.substack.com/), and started focussing on 1-2 [ideas](https://winnny.com) I’d normally have parked. All while doing the day job, without feeling 100% most weeks, and with a list of ‘proper priorities’ that never got shorter. If I’d waited for the conditions to be right, none of it would exist.

Here’s what actually happened instead: I stopped treating creating as something that needed a dedicated block of time, and started treating it as something that fits into the moments I already had. Ideas scribbled down on a walk. A rough version of a tool built over a few evenings instead of one big weekend sprint. Five minutes of iterating between projects. 

None of those moments felt like ‘working on the business’. They felt more like small, low-stakes bets on an idea. That’s actually exactly why they worked. There was no pressure to make each one count, so I actually did them.

A single micro moment doesn’t do much on its own. Thirty minutes of poking around at an idea is sort of... nothing. But five, fifteen, thirty minutes, repeated over months, without insisting “I need a proper run at this“, compounds into something real.

[Winnny.com](https://winnny.com/) started that way. So did [Hndmark.com](https://hndmark.com/). Neither began as a long-term plan. They began as small, half-formed ideas I kept iterating on. No calendar space was cleared for them.

Waiting for the right moment seems like it’s the efficient thing to do. You’re not spending, so it’s not costing you anything. 

Wrong. Waiting actually does have a cost. It’s not just the cost of delay, it’s that you’re starting from nothing when ‘the moment’ finally arrives. You don’t have a half-built version to react to, or a sense of whether the idea even holds up. You certainly don’t have momentum.

Plant the seeds early and by the time you’re ‘ready’ you're not starting cold. You’re picking up something that already has foundations. A rough prototype, a page of notes, maybe an audience of a dozen people who’ve been watching you think out loud. That head start is worth more than the extra hour a day you were holding out for.

I still don’t have more time than anyone else. I’m still not always runnning at 100%. The list of priorities hasn’t shrunk. What’s changed is that I stopped treating ‘not enough space’ as a reason to not start.

If there’s an idea you keep circling back to, please stop waiting for the version of your life where you’ve got a free day/week/month to give it. 

Give it thirty minutes today. Then again tomorrow, or next week. Let it sit half-finished for a while if it needs to. Park it in your box of ideas. 

The point isn’t to finish it in the micro moments. The point is to make sure that when you’re finally ready to invest in one (or many) ideas, you’re not starting from zero.
